## How to say Swahili

Swahili is one of the easiest languages to read aloud: it is spelled the way
it sounds, and every letter is said.

<ul>
<li><strong>The vowels</strong> are always the same: <em>a</em> as in "father", <em>e</em> as in "bed", <em>i</em> as in "machine", <em>o</em> as in "more", <em>u</em> as in "rule".</li>
<li><strong>The stress</strong> falls on the syllable before last - <em>a-SAN-te</em>, <em>ha-BA-ri</em>. The guide under each phrase puts it in capitals.</li>
<li><strong>dh</strong> is the <em>th</em> in "this", <strong>th</strong> the <em>th</em> in "thin", and <strong>gh</strong> a soft, throaty <em>g</em>.</li>
<li><strong>m</strong> or <strong>n</strong> before another consonant is said as its own short hum - <em>mbili</em> is "m-BEE-lee", <em>nzuri</em> "n-ZOO-ree".</li>
<li><strong>ng'</strong> with an apostrophe is the <em>ng</em> in "singer"; <strong>ng</strong> without one is the <em>ng</em> in "finger".</li>
</ul>

## Swahili or Sheng?

**Swahili** is Kenya's national language, and with English one of its two
official ones. Almost everyone in Nairobi speaks it, whatever their first
language, and it is what you will hear in matatus, markets and offices
alike.

**Sheng** is Nairobi's street slang: Swahili grammar with words from English,
Kikuyu, Luo and others, invented and abandoned fast. It is used mostly by the
young and in informal places. This book gives Sheng only where a word has
lasted for years - {{ PHRASEBOOK.sheng_count }} of the {{ PHRASEBOOK.count }}
phrases - and it is fine to stick to Swahili. A visitor's Sheng is usually
met with laughter, mostly the friendly kind.

## Swahili time

The one thing that catches out everybody: in Swahili the day starts at
dawn. **Saa moja** - hour one - is 7am, the first hour of daylight, and
**saa sita** is noon. To turn Swahili time into the clock, add six hours (or
take six away). Most Nairobians switch to the clock when they speak English,
but if someone says *saa mbili* in Swahili, they mean 8 o'clock, not 2.
