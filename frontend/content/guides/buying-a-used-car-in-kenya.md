Most cars on Kenyan roads are bought second-hand - either imported used
from Japan, the UK or elsewhere, or sold locally. A used car can be good
value, but fraud and hidden problems are common. These checks protect you.

## Where to look

- **Car yards and dealers** - along Ngong Road, Mombasa Road and elsewhere
  in Nairobi, selling locally used and freshly imported cars.
- **Online marketplaces** and social media groups.
- **Private sellers** - friends, colleagues and people leaving the country,
  often with full service records.
- **Importing one yourself** - see [Importing a car to Kenya](/guides/importing-a-car-to-kenya)
  and the [car import duty calculator](/tools/car-import-duty-calculator).

## 1. Search the car on NTSA

Before paying anything, get the car's **copy of records** from the
**National Transport and Safety Authority** (NTSA) on **eCitizen**. It costs
**KES {{ NTSA_COPY_OF_RECORDS_FEE|shillings }}**. You need the registration
or chassis number. The search shows:

- the **registered owner**
- the **make, model, year**, chassis and engine numbers
- whether there is a **caveat** - usually because a bank or lender financed
  the car and still has an interest in it

Use only the official NTSA service on eCitizen: NTSA has warned about fake
websites offering vehicle searches.

## 2. Match the car to the records

- The seller should be the **registered owner**, or have written authority
  from them. Check their ID against the records.
- Check the **chassis and engine numbers** on the car match the records
  and the logbook. A mismatch can mean a stolen or rebuilt car, and an
  insurer will not cover it.
- If there is a **caveat**, do not pay until the seller shows a letter from
  the financier, and the caveat is lifted.

## 3. Inspect it

- Have a **trusted mechanic** inspect it, or use a paid inspection service.
- Check for accident repairs, rust, leaks, worn tyres and warning lights.
- **Test drive** on different roads.
- Ask for **service records** and receipts.
- Check the **mileage** is plausible for the car's age and condition.

## 4. Agree and pay safely

- Write a **sale agreement** with both parties' details, the car's details,
  the price, and that the car is sold free of debts and caveats. An advocate
  can draft one for larger amounts.
- Pay by **bank transfer** or banker's cheque, not cash.
- Do not hand over the full payment before the **transfer** is started.

## 5. Transfer ownership

The seller starts the transfer on the NTSA portal on eCitizen, and you
accept it and pay the transfer fee. See
[Transferring a car logbook](/guides/transferring-a-car-logbook). Kenya is
moving to digital logbooks, so check the current process on eCitizen.

## 6. Insure it

Insure the car **before you drive it away**. Third-party insurance is the
legal minimum. See [Car insurance in Kenya](/guides/car-insurance-in-kenya).

## Warning signs

- A price well below similar cars.
- A seller who will not meet at their home or office, or hurries you.
- Logbook details that do not match, or "the logbook is with the bank, but
  it is fine".
- Requests to pay a deposit to hold the car before you have seen it.
