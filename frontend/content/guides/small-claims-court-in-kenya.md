If someone owes you money - a client who will not pay, a landlord keeping
your deposit, a seller who took payment and never delivered - the **Small
Claims Court** is a quick, cheap way to get a decision. You do not need a
lawyer.

## What it hears

Claims of up to **KES {{ SMALL_CLAIMS_LIMIT|shillings }}** about:

- unpaid money - debts, loans, invoices
- **contracts** for goods or services that were not honoured
- **damage to property**, including from road accidents
- **deposits** and rent

You cannot split a bigger claim into several small ones to fit the limit.
Land disputes, employment claims and family matters go to other courts.

## Before you file

1. **Write a demand letter** to the other side setting out what they owe,
   why, and a deadline to pay - for example, fourteen days. Many pay at this
   stage.
2. **Gather your evidence** - contracts, invoices, receipts, M-Pesa
   statements, messages, photos.
3. **Know who you are claiming against** - the person's or company's full,
   correct name and an address where they can be served.

## Filing a claim

- Fill in the **statement of claim** (form SCC 1), available at the court
  and on the Judiciary's website.
- File it at the **Small Claims Court registry** - in Nairobi, at the
  Milimani Law Courts - or through the Judiciary's **e-filing** system, and
  pay the filing fee. Fees are low; check the current amount at the
  registry.
- You must then **serve** the claim on the other side, and file proof that
  you did.

## The hearing

- You can represent yourself. Proceedings are **informal** and can be in
  English or Kiswahili.
- The court can decide on documents alone, or hold a short hearing, in
  person or online.
- Bring originals and copies of your evidence, and any witnesses.
- The court encourages **settlement**; you can agree terms at any point.

The law requires the court to decide a claim within
**{{ SMALL_CLAIMS_DECISION_DAYS }} days** of filing.

## Getting paid

If you win, the court orders the other side to pay, often within a set
time. If they still do not, you can ask the court to **enforce** the
order - for example by attaching their property or bank account.

## Appeals

Either side can appeal to the **High Court**, but only on points of law, not
on the facts.
