Money market funds (MMFs) are where Kenyans keep savings that should earn
more than a bank account but stay within reach. You can start with a few
hundred shillings, add money by [[M-Pesa]], earn interest every day, and
usually have your money back in two or three working days.

They have grown into one of Kenya's biggest ways to save: about
**KES {{ MMF_INDUSTRY_ASSETS_BN }} billion** sat in {{ MMF_FUND_COUNT }}
money market funds at the end of June 2026, around half of all the money in
unit trusts. This guide explains how they work and how to choose one. It is
general information, not a recommendation of any fund.

## What a money market fund is

An MMF is a **unit trust**: many investors pool their money, and a
professional **fund manager** invests it in short-term, low-risk debt -
mostly **Treasury bills**, **fixed deposits** at banks, and short-term
**corporate paper**. The interest those earn, less the manager's fee, is
shared out among investors every day.

Three parties keep it honest:

- the **fund manager**, licensed by the **Capital Markets Authority (CMA)**,
  decides what to buy
- a **custodian**, usually a bank, holds the fund's money and investments -
  not the manager
- a **trustee** checks that the manager follows the rules and acts for
  investors

Shilling MMFs usually keep each unit at **KES 1**, so your balance simply
grows by the interest added. See
[How money market fund interest works](/guides/how-money-market-fund-interest-works).

## How to compare funds

**1. Before or after tax.** Every fund deducts
{{ INTEREST_WHT_RATE|percent }}% **withholding tax** from interest, and funds
advertise the yield **before** it. A fund advertising 10% pays you about 8.5%.
Compare every fund on the same basis.

**2. Effective annual yield.** Funds quote an effective annual yield, which
assumes interest is compounded. Some apps also show a daily yield; don't
compare one fund's daily figure with another's annual one.

**3. Consistency, not one week's rate.** The top of the table changes every
month. A fund that has stayed in the top third for a year tells you more
than a spike in one week.

**4. Fees.** Most of the funds whose documents we checked charge about
**2% a year**, already taken out of the yield they publish. Look instead for
fees on **withdrawals** - some funds give one free withdrawal a month and
charge for more - and for any **ledger** or account fees.

**5. Withdrawals.** Most pay out within one to three working days, to M-Pesa,
a bank, or both. Check limits, the minimum withdrawal, and whether
withdrawals after a cut-off time wait a day.

**6. Minimum to start.** Many funds start at KES 100 or KES 1,000; some ask
for KES 5,000 or more. The comparer shows the minimums funds confirm.

**7. Size and manager.** Big funds run by long-established managers cope
better when many investors withdraw at once. Small, new funds sometimes buy
riskier paper to reach the top of the table.

## Is my money safe?

MMFs are among the safest investments in Kenya, but **not guaranteed**.

- They are **not covered by deposit insurance**, unlike bank deposits.
- A fund can lose money if a company or bank it lent to cannot pay - which
  is why what it holds matters. Funds publish their holdings in monthly
  **factsheets**: mostly government paper is the safest mix.
- Your money is held by the **custodian**, separate from the manager's own
  money, so if the manager collapses the fund's assets are still there.
- Use only funds **approved by the CMA**, which publishes the list of
  approved collective investment schemes.

Be wary of anything that calls itself an MMF but promises a fixed, high
return, or cannot show a CMA approval. See
[Crypto and investment scams](/guides/crypto-and-investment-scams-in-kenya).

## Tax

The {{ INTEREST_WHT_RATE|percent }}% withholding tax on MMF interest is a
**final tax** for resident individuals: the fund deducts it and pays KRA,
and there is nothing more to pay. Many funds send an annual tax certificate.
Some differently structured and offshore funds are taxed differently - ask
before you assume.

## Shilling or dollar?

Dollar MMFs pay less but hold their value if the shilling falls. They suit
people who earn, spend or plan to spend in dollars - school fees abroad,
travel, a dollar loan. See
[Dollar money market funds in Kenya](/guides/dollar-money-market-funds-in-kenya).

## Shariah-compliant funds

Several managers run **Shariah-compliant** funds, which avoid
interest-bearing paper and invest in Islamic instruments instead, with
returns described as profit rather than interest. Ask for the fund's Shariah
board and how it screens investments. Ziidi, on the M-Pesa menu, has a
Shariah version too.

## Opening one

1. Choose a fund, and download its app or go to its website or office.
2. Register with your **ID or passport**, your **KRA PIN** and bank details.
   Foreigners living in Kenya usually need a foreigner ID or passport and a
   KRA PIN.
3. Deposit by **M-Pesa paybill** or bank transfer, using your account number
   as the reference.
4. Check your statement or app: interest should appear daily.
5. **Name a beneficiary or next of kin**, so the money can reach your family
   without a long [succession](/guides/death-and-succession-in-kenya) case.

On **Ziidi**, opened from the M-Pesa menu, you invest straight from your
M-Pesa wallet from KES 100, without a separate app.

## Ways people use MMFs

- **An emergency fund** - three to six months of spending, earning interest
  but reachable in days.
- **Saving for a goal** - a rent deposit, school fees, a plot - with
  automatic monthly top-ups.
- **A chama's money** - many funds open group accounts with several
  signatories.
- **Parking money** between other investments, such as Treasury bills
  maturing.

For growth over many years, MMFs on their own usually lag
[shares](/guides/investing-on-the-nse), [bonds](/guides/buying-treasury-bills-and-bonds)
and [pensions](/guides/pensions-in-kenya).
